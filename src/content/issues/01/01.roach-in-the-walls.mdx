The building is ninety years old and it does not pretend otherwise. The floors
run downhill toward the bay window. The radiators knock twice before dawn like
someone politely asking to be let in. And on the third night, while I was
standing in the kitchen waiting for the kettle, a roach the size of a house key
walked out from behind the stove, stopped, and regarded me with what I can only
describe as ownership.

I did not scream. I want that on the record. I put the kettle down and we looked
at each other for a while, the way you look at a stranger who has turned out to
be a roommate.

## The lease you don't sign

Every old apartment comes with tenants who predate you. Silverfish in the
bathroom grout. A spider in the high corner of the stairwell who has clearly
been managing that territory for generations. Pigeons with opinions. You sign a
piece of paper at a desk somewhere and you believe this makes the place yours,
and then you move your boxes in and discover you have joined an ecosystem that
was doing fine without you.

The roach — I started calling him the Super, because he seemed to be doing
rounds — kept a schedule. Ten forty at night, out from behind the stove, a slow
circuit of the baseboard, a pause under the radiator, gone by eleven. I began to
find it reassuring. If the Super was doing his rounds, the building was still
standing. If the building was still standing, so was I.

> You do not get to decide, on your own, what a home is for. The home has been
> accumulating purposes since before you arrived.

There is a version of this essay that is about extermination, and I understand
why you might expect it. That is the genre. Person moves into old building,
person does battle with old building, person wins or loses and either way learns
something tidy about resilience. I set out to write that one. I bought the
traps. I read the forums, which are their own kind of horror, full of people
speaking about their apartments the way generals speak about contested valleys.

## What the traps taught me

I put down the traps and then I could not stop thinking about them. Not out of
guilt exactly — more that the traps changed the feeling of the rooms. Every
corner became a site of intended violence. I would be reading on the couch and
remember that under the couch, six inches from my heel, something was designed
to be happening slowly.

So I took them up again. This is not a principled position and I am not
recommending it. It is just that I would rather live in a building that has a
Super doing rounds than a building that has a number of small machines waiting
in the dark. The first is a place where things live. The second is a place where
things are prevented from living, which is a different address entirely, and I
have lived at that address before and did not like who I was there.

The knocking radiators, it turns out, are air in the pipes, and there is a valve
you can bleed to stop it, and I have chosen not to. The downhill floors mean a
dropped orange rolls all the way to the window and waits there in the light. The
static from downstairs comes up through the boards around nine and I have
learned the downstairs television's whole weekly schedule without ever seeing
it, the way you learn a neighbour's car.

And the Super still comes out at ten forty. I have stopped waiting for him
specifically but I notice when the circuit happens, the same way you notice the
last light going off in a window across the street. Somebody else is up. Somebody
else is doing their rounds. The building holds.

## A first dispatch

I am going to keep writing these. Not about the roach, necessarily — about the
household, which is bigger than the people in it and always has been. The plants
that came with opinions about the light. The particular draft that only exists
in February. The version of me that this set of rooms is slowly producing, who
is more patient than the last one and keeps the kettle full.

Welcome to Roachworld. It was here before the name.
